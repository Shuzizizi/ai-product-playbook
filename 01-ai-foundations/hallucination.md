# Hallucination (in AI/LLMs)

## a. Background & Why
Hallucination is when an AI model generates output that is factually wrong, fabricated, or unsupported by its source data — but states it fluently and confidently, as if it were true. It happens because LLMs are fundamentally predicting "what text plausibly comes next," not looking up verified facts in a database. When the model doesn't actually know an answer, it doesn't reliably say "I don't know" — it often generates something statistically plausible instead. This matters for products because confident-sounding wrong answers are more dangerous than obviously wrong ones — users tend to trust them.

## b. Life Before It (Traditional Way) vs Now
**Before:** Traditional software either returned a correct, deterministic result or a clear error/no-result state ("record not found"). There was no concept of a system being *confidently wrong* — bugs produced crashes or nulls, not persuasive fiction.

**Now:** A generative system can produce a fluent, well-formatted, entirely fabricated answer — a fake citation, a nonexistent API method, a wrong statistic — with the same tone of confidence as a correct one. Teams now have to design specifically for this new failure mode.

## c. What Changes/Differs Without It
If hallucination weren't a risk, teams could deploy LLM output directly with far less friction — no fact-checking layer, no citation requirements, no disclaimers. Because hallucination is real, products working with LLMs must add guardrails: grounding, citations, human review, or confidence signaling, which adds cost and complexity but is necessary for trust and safety.

## d. Benefits (of Understanding/Managing Hallucination)
- Understanding this failure mode lets teams design safer products (e.g., "show sources," "flag uncertainty")
- Encourages grounding techniques (retrieval-augmented generation) that improve real accuracy
- Drives better evaluation practices instead of blind trust in AI output
- Helps set appropriate user expectations, reducing over-reliance and misuse

## e. Limitations
- Cannot currently be fully eliminated in generative models — only reduced or mitigated
- Harder to catch in domains where the reviewer isn't already an expert
- Grounding/citation techniques add latency, cost, and engineering complexity
- Users often don't verify AI output themselves, especially under time pressure

## f. Real-Life & Business Examples
- **Legal:** A U.S. lawyer submitted a court brief with fake case citations generated by an LLM, resulting in sanctions — a well-known cautionary case.
- **Customer service:** An airline's chatbot invented a refund policy that didn't exist; the company was later held responsible for honoring it.
- **Coding assistants:** LLMs sometimes invent plausible-looking library functions or APIs that don't actually exist, breaking builds if not checked.
- **Search/Q&A products:** Early AI search summaries have cited sources that didn't say what was claimed, damaging user trust.

## g. What This Means for an IT Product Owner
- Never ship unreviewed LLM output in high-stakes contexts (legal, medical, financial, safety) without human sign-off.
- Prioritize grounding (retrieval-augmented generation, verified data sources) over relying on the model's raw internal knowledge for factual claims.
- Design the UI to communicate uncertainty — citations, confidence indicators, "verify before acting" prompts — rather than presenting all AI output as equally authoritative.
- Define an incident response process for when hallucinated output causes real harm (wrong info given to a customer, etc.) — assume it will happen.
- Include hallucination-rate testing as part of your QA/acceptance criteria for any generative feature, not just functional testing.
